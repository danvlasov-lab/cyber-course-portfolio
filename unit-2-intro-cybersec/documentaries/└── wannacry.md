## Findings

### 1. The incident in my own words

The WannaCry attack started on 12 May 2017 and spread very fast around the world. It was ransomware that locked people's files and asked them to pay money. About 200,000 computers in more than 150 countries were affected, including hospitals, companies and government organizations. What surprised me most was how quickly it could move from one computer to another without waiting for users to do something.

### 2. Who was affected, and how

Many organizations were affected by WannaCry. One of the biggest problems was in the UK National Health Service (NHS), where hospitals could not use their computer systems normally. Some appointments were cancelled and some patients had to go to other hospitals. This shows that a cyberattack can affect real people, not only computers.

### 3. The CIA principle

I think **availability** was the main part of the CIA triad affected by WannaCry. People could not open their files or use their computers normally. The files were also changed because they were encrypted, so **integrity** was affected too. However, the biggest problem was that the information and systems were not available to users.

### 4. The attack technique - ransomware and "wormable" exploits

Ransomware is malware that locks or encrypts files and asks people to pay money. WannaCry was more dangerous because it could spread by itself using the EternalBlue vulnerability. Normal ransomware often needs a person to click on something, but WannaCry could move from one computer to another automatically. This made the attack spread much faster and affected many organizations.

### 5. How was it discovered and how was it stopped

People noticed the attack when their computers stopped working normally and showed a message asking for money. The message said that the files had been encrypted and that the user needed to pay Bitcoin to get them back. A security researcher called Marcus Hutchins found that WannaCry checked a specific website before continuing its work. He registered the domain, which activated the kill switch and stopped most new infections, although computers that were already infected stayed infected.

### 6. What could have helped - the patch question

Microsoft had released a security update before the WannaCry attack, but many organizations did not install it. One reason was that some organizations used old computer systems that were more difficult to update. Blocking the network connection used by WannaCry could also have helped to stop it from spreading. Regular updates would have been one of the most important protections because the vulnerability used by EternalBlue already had a patch.

### 7. The broader lesson

WannaCry showed me that software updates are very important for security. Old and unpatched computers can cause big problems, especially in hospitals and other important organizations. I was also surprised that a tool connected with government hacking capabilities was later leaked and used in a worldwide attack. This shows how dangerous it can be when powerful cyber tools get into the wrong hands.

### 8. My personal takeaway

The most interesting thing for me was that some organizations could not simply update their old systems. I now understand that software updates are an important part of protecting my devices from threats. I will try to keep my computer and other devices updated and make more backups to protect my data. My main takeaway is that keeping systems updated can prevent serious problems.
